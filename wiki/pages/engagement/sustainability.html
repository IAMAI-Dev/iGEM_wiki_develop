    Overview

    "If people do not let down the green mountains, the green mountains will surely not let down the people."

    What is sustainable development?

    Sustainable development is defined as development that "meets the needs of the present generation without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs" (UN Brundtland Commission, 1987). It is not a single environmental issue, but rather a systemic framework encompassing three dimensions: environmental health, economic viability, and social equity.

    In 2015, the 193 member states of the United Nations adopted the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development, establishing 17 Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) that set a common global agenda for action in areas such as poverty reduction, health, education, clean energy, environmental protection, and responsible consumption and production. These 17 goals are interconnected and indivisible — progress in one area often depends on coordinated advances in others.

    How is our project related to it?

    Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) are a class of emerging pollutants widely generated in industrial production and everyday life — from coking plant wastewater to barbecue-stall fumes, from automobile exhaust to cigarette smoke. These pollutants persist in water bodies, soils, and the atmosphere, exhibiting carcinogenic, teratogenic, and mutagenic effects — so-called "three-cause" toxicity — which has led many countries to classify them as priority controlled pollutants; China's "Action Plan for the Control of Emerging Pollutants" also designates these substances as key targets for regulation.

    However, the current state of governance is far from optimistic. Existing regulatory approaches rely heavily on laboratory-based chromatography-mass spectrometry, which is costly and time-consuming; handheld rapid-testing devices can only provide vague qualitative results, with data lacking legal validity, thereby perpetuating the longstanding dilemma of "having standards but struggling to enforce them." Meanwhile, PAH sources have long permeated everyday life — barbecue stalls, congested roadways, coal-fired heating — implying that effective management cannot depend solely on a handful of large laboratories. Instead, it calls for a more streamlined, widely accessible response system.

    Our response: a dual-strain time-difference sequential strategy. Samples are first incubated with the degrading bacteria; within 1–2 hours, naphthalene is degraded into salicylic acid. The reaction mixture is then transferred to a sensor-bacterial assay tube for incubation, and within about 30 minutes, whether the naphthalene concentration has fallen below the remediation standard is determined by checking whether fluorescence intensity has reached a preset threshold.

    SDG 3 — Good Health

    What did we do?

    The sources of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons are far more extensive than commonly believed. In addition to industrial sources such as coking plants and fuel depots, everyday activities — including cooking fumes from barbecue stalls, vehicle exhaust, cigarette smoke, and household coal-based heating — also continuously generate PAHs. This means that the risk of PAHs exposure is not limited to industrial workers — it has already permeated the everyday breathing and diet of ordinary people.

    Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, including benzo[a]pyrene and other multi-component mixtures, have been classified by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) as Group 1 or Group 2B carcinogens. Workers at coking plants, fuel storage facilities, and chemical factories are chronically exposed to PAHs, while nearby communities are also affected through contaminated water and air.

    Our approach safeguards health through two pathways:

    • Monitoring and Early Warning: Fluorescent signals are visible to the naked eye, eliminating the need for expensive equipment. Workers or regulatory personnel can readily determine whether PAH concentrations exceed permissible limits and promptly initiate emergency measures such as ventilation, use of protective gear, and process adjustments. In everyday settings like barbecue stalls and residential areas, frontline inspectors can also use this tool to rapidly assess PAH exposure levels in public spaces, addressing the current gap in rapid screening methods for PAHs from domestic sources.
    • Source-level reduction: Degrading microorganisms convert naphthalene into harmless end products, offering the potential to lower the pollution load at its source and thereby mitigate the risk of chronic exposure in downstream drinking water sources and agricultural soils. China's Action Plan for the Control of Emerging Pollutants explicitly calls for prioritized management of persistent organic pollutants. In local practice, the Yangzhou Chemical Industrial Park in Jiangsu Province has reduced occupational exposure risks from toxic chemicals at the source by redesigning its material-handling systems and implementing multi-stage purification and recycling — demonstrating the feasibility of the "source-level exposure reduction" approach in real-world industrial settings. It should be noted that degradation formulations, as long-term reserve products, are currently not being promoted due to restrictions under policies governing the environmental release of engineered microorganisms; at present, commercial efforts are focused on diagnostic test kits.

    SDG 3's goal is to "ensure healthy lives and promote well-being for all at all ages." We believe that prevention is the most effective safeguard for health and well-being — and the prerequisite for prevention is the ability to transform invisible pollutants into visible data, quickly and at a sufficiently low cost.

    This further reinforces our commitment to make testing affordable and accessible enough so that even small businesses and resource-limited regions can conduct routine PAHs screening, thereby reducing occupational exposure and community health risks.

    SDG 6 — Clean Water

    What did we do?

    Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) are a class of persistent organic pollutants frequently detected in wastewater from coking plants, gasworks, and chemical industrial parks. A review article on PAH remediation published on ScienceDirect highlights that one of the key bottlenecks in current PAH management is the transition from "advocacy" to "practical implementation" — particularly in areas such as bioavailability assessment, standardized monitoring protocols, and low-carbon accounting for remediation technologies, where systematic solutions remain lacking [1]. To address this challenge, we selected naphthalene, a two-ring compound, as a model pollutant and developed a two-strain system with sequential operation: the first step involves incubation of degrading bacteria (converting naphthalene to salicylic acid within 1–2 hours), followed by the second step, in which sensing bacteria detect the target compound, yielding a fluorescent signal in about 30 minutes.

    Stepwise operation for detection and degradation: We employ a two-strain, time-differential sequential strategy. In the first step, the sample is incubated with degrading bacteria for 1–2 hours, allowing naphthalene to be converted into salicylic acid by the naphthalene-hydroxylating enzyme in the degraders. In the second step, the reaction mixture is transferred to a sensing-bacterial assay tube and incubated for 30 minutes; the salicylic acid-responsive regulatory sequence carried by the sensor strain is activated, driving the expression of a fluorescent protein. Fluorescence intensity is directly proportional to salicylic acid concentration, enabling rapid, on-site compliance assessment of PAHs. It should be noted that the complete mineralization pathway of the degrading bacteria — naphthalene → salicylic acid → gentisic acid → TCA cycle → CO₂ and H₂O — is currently being developed as a long-term reserve technology. Due to restrictions imposed by policies governing the environmental release of engineered microorganisms, this capability is not being commercialized at this time and will be advanced only after a clear regulatory pathway has been established.

    Preliminary tests indicate that, throughout the entire process, this kit can generate test results within 2–3 hours, with the cost of a single test kept between several dozen and one hundred RMB — significantly reduced compared to traditional GC-MS/HPLC methods.

    As SDG 6 calls for — "Ensure availability and sustainable management of water and sanitation for all" — we believe that making detection technologies affordable and accessible is, in itself, an essential component of sustainable water resource management.

    Feedback from stakeholders

    "At present, quantitative testing is largely conducted by third-party laboratories using gas chromatography or liquid chromatography, and the entire process — from sample collection to report generation — can be quite time-consuming. For small and micro enterprises, this poses a particularly heavy cost burden."

    — Li Qiqi, Huangpi District Bureau of Ecology and Environment, Wuhan

    She also pointed out that existing PID/FID handheld detectors are "fast, but not accurate enough" — usable, but without legal force at the law-enforcement level.

    This feedback directly defined our design objective: to develop a low-cost, portable biosensor to serve as a laboratory "outpost" — by employing high-coverage, low-cost primary screening, we can allocate our limited testing resources precisely to where they are most needed. And our engineered bacteria's unique characteristic — the stepwise synergistic action of two strains — means that pollution reduction is initiated even during the screening process, an added value that conventional rapid-testing devices simply cannot offer.

    SDG 15 — Life on Land

    What did we do?

    Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon pollution can also enter soils through leaks, irrigation with wastewater, and atmospheric deposition, reducing microbial diversity, inhibiting seed germination, disrupting nutrient cycling, and ultimately leading to land degradation and biodiversity loss.

    Our engineered microbial strains are naturally suited for in-situ soil bioremediation:

    • Rapid preliminary screening: Whole-cell biosensors can be directly applied to soil leachate or groundwater samples, eliminating the need for complex sample pretreatment. Within 2–3 hours, preliminary screening results for pollution levels can be obtained, helping to quickly assess the level of pollution.
    • Synchronous signal generation for detection: During the assay, naphthalene in the sample is converted into salicylic acid by naphthalene-hydroxylating enzymes in the degrading bacteria, and this salicylic acid simultaneously serves as the detection signal molecule for the sensor strain. We place particular emphasis on comprehensive pathway metabolite analysis (GC-MS) to validate the complete metabolic route — from naphthalene through salicylic acid to gentisic acid and ultimately to the TCA cycle — ensuring that no toxic intermediates accumulate; this represents a critical safety threshold we rigorously uphold. It should be noted that degradation formulations based on this engineered strain are currently held as a long-term reserve and, due to regulatory constraints on the environmental release of genetically modified organisms, are not being marketed at this time. They will be made available as commercial products once compliant pathways have been clearly established.
    • Biosafety containment: Biosafety is a prerequisite for soil applications. We employ immobilized microspheres or kit-based containment systems to ensure that engineered microorganisms do not proliferate or spread in the environment; at the same time, we design auxotrophic strains that undergo programmed cell death once removed from laboratory conditions, thereby eliminating the risks of horizontal gene transfer and ecological invasion.

    Feedback from stakeholders

    "Many teams track only the parent compound. However, the degradation of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons can stall at intermediate products, which sometimes exhibit greater toxicity and mobility. Comprehensive pathway validation is essential."

    — Professor Xinli Xing, China University of Geosciences (Wuhan)

    Professor Xing also pointed out that naphthalene is widely distributed in the natural environment and exhibits complex environmental behavior, yet existing conventional risk assessment frameworks pay relatively little attention to low-ring PAHs. This precisely demonstrates that establishing a comprehensive detection and degradation technology platform, with naphthalene as the breakthrough point, is essential for filling a weak link in the PAHs governance chain, and is of critical preliminary significance. Guided by this principle, we will conduct a comprehensive metabolite-profile analysis to confirm the complete mineralization of naphthalene into carbon dioxide and water, ensuring that, building on our success with naphthalene, our approach can be extended — using the same technical rationale — to other PAH contaminants, thereby truly remediating soils without causing secondary harm.

    "Whole-cell biosensors involve living engineered microorganisms. In field or industrial settings, the microbial cells must be contained within a closed system — for example, developed into a kit or immobilized microspheres — to avoid environmental release. Do we need to apply for an environmental release permit? Do we need to inform the public? These are not merely technical issues; they also concern trust."

    — Li Qiqi, Huangpi District Bureau of Ecology and Environment, Wuhan

    This makes biosafety and closed-loop systems an uncompromising design priority for our project.

    SDG 17 — Partnerships for the Goals

    What did we do?

    No single team can solve environmental pollution on its own. From the very beginning of its design, our project has proactively established a cross-sectoral collaboration network, bringing together academia, regulatory agencies, industry, community organizations, and the iGEM community.

    • Academia: Professor Xinli Xing of China University of Geosciences (Wuhan) provided expert guidance on the environmental behavior of PAHs, field sampling design, validation of degradation pathways, and gaps in the policy framework, directly helping us define a comprehensive technical roadmap and safety standards for the entire process — from detection to complete mineralization — of naphthalene.
    • Regulatory agencies: Li Qiqi of the Huangpi District Branch, Wuhan Municipal Ecology and Environment Bureau, highlighted the real pain points of oversight — high costs, lengthy timelines, and the lack of legally recognized rapid-testing tools — and proposed a tiered regulatory approach of "on-site rapid testing plus laboratory confirmation." She also emphasized that successful implementation must overcome three critical hurdles: accuracy, method validation, and biosafety.
    • Community: In Chenzhou, Hunan Province, Director Wu Xianglian of the He Xiaoqing Action Center provided field observations of river PAHs pollution, making the need for low-cost, easy-to-use tools in grassroots river-monitoring efforts both tangible and pressing.
    • Industry (engineering): Engineers from Wuhan Zhongke Shuicheng offered recommendations on practical operational constraints — such as scale-up cultivation of engineered microorganisms, selection of immobilization materials, and field-transport conditions — helping us anticipate in advance the real-world challenges that may arise when transitioning from the laboratory to field applications.
    • Industry (commercialization): Director Han of Yuangou Bio (Wuhan) Co., Ltd. cautioned us from a commercialization perspective: there is a significant gap between academic thinking and industrial thinking, one defined by "cost" and "compliance." His feedback prompted us to abandon the complex "three-in-one" design and return to the product's core — separating the detection and degradation functions and focusing on threshold-based determination of "compliant/non-compliant." Under existing regulatory constraints, we were able to launch a compliant testing product while preserving long-term commercial potential for degradation formulations.
    • iGEM community: We also collaborated with other iGEM teams through experience sharing — collaborative iteration within the iGEM community enhances the robustness of each project.

    Feedback from stakeholders

    Our dialogue with multiple stakeholders has conveyed a consistent message:

    "Studying the migration of pollutants requires analyzing key parameters such as the octanol-water partition coefficient, the organic phase-atmosphere partition coefficient, and Henry's law constant — there is no universal, one-size-fits-all rule. Field sampling must account for land-use type, seasonal temperature, and soil organic matter content."

    — Professor Xinli Xing, China University of Geosciences (Wuhan)

    This directly informed our sampling design and experimental validation plan.

    "For a biosensor to be adopted by regulators, it must clear three hurdles: first, accuracy — its performance must be on par with standard methods; second, method validation — only then will the data carry legal weight; and third, biosafety — requiring a closed-system design. These are not optional; they are prerequisites."

    — Li Qiqi, Huangpi District Bureau of Ecology and Environment, Wuhan

    This provides us with a clear technical roadmap: in the short term, develop an on-site screening tool for identifying soil contamination risks, and in the long term, achieve high accuracy and secure method validation.

    "No matter how advanced the technology, it's meaningless if it stays confined to the lab. You need to understand who will use it, who will oversee it, and who will be affected. Involve everyone as early as possible."

    — Representative of a local environmental NGO

    These insights have shaped our project from the very outset, rather than being tacked on afterward. Our approach will be designed to be scientifically sound, regulatorily viable, ecologically safe, and socially acceptable.
