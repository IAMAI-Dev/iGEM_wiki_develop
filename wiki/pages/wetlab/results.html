    Module 1: Construction and Optimization of the Salicylic Acid Sensing Pathway

    1.1Performance Characterization of the Initial Salicylic‑Acid Biosensor

    In this round, the plasmid pUC57‑Pr‑NahR(E169/C248)‑Psal‑eGFP was successfully constructed, achieving heterologous expression of the sensing circuit in Escherichia coli. Using E. coli DH5α/pUC57‑Psal‑eGFP and blank DH5α strain as dual controls, fluorescence detection under gradient salicylic acid induction showed that neither E. coli DH5α nor E. coli DH5α/pUC57‑Psal‑eGFP produced any response in the presence of salicylic acid. This result confirmed that salicylic‑acid binding to NahR is required to activate the Psal promoter for initiating transcription and driving the expression of enhanced green fluorescent protein (eGFP).

    Figure 1: Initial Limit of Detection of the Biosensor

    Host‑screening experiments demonstrated that compared with XL10-gold, DH10B and Top10 strains, E. coli DH5α exhibited higher fluorescence intensity and better signal stability, making it the optimal host for sensing assays. Time‑course assays revealed that the fluorescence signal increased most markedly after 10 h of salicylic acid induction; therefore, 10 h was set as the standard detection duration. The original wild‑type sensing system had a limit of detection (LOD) of 100 μM for salicylic acid, with overall low sensing sensitivity.

    Figure 3: Time- and concentration-dependent response curves of the SA whole-cell biosensor

    1.2 Promoter Modification Improves Basal Sensing Sensitivity

    Site‑directed mutagenesis was performed on the core conserved regions of the Pr and Psal promoters to obtain the MUT1st strain. Performance tests showed that the promoter‑modified strain generated obvious fluorescent responses at 20 μM salicylic acid. Compared with the wild‑type system, the limit of detection for salicylic acid decreased from 100 μM to 20 μM, representing a 5‑fold improvement in sensing sensitivity. Moreover, stable dose‑dependent responses were observed across gradient salicylic‑acid concentrations.

    1.3 Screening of High‑Sensitivity Sensing Mutants via Saturated Mutagenesis of NahR Protein

    A dual‑site saturated mutagenesis library targeting E169 and C248 of the NahR protein was constructed based on the MUT1st strain. Six plates were transformed with approximately 240 transformants per plate, yielding roughly 1400 transformants in total, which was more than three times the theoretical value of 400. Accordingly, the saturated mutant library covered all theoretical mutation combinations. Sequencing results showed that the 169‑ and 248‑amino‑acid residues of the NahR protein were mutated to distinct amino acids in plasmids from 9 transformants, corresponding to a mutagenesis efficiency of 90 %, which verified the successful construction of the mutant library. Through plate‑based primary screening and low‑concentration liquid‑culture secondary screening, the superior mutant No. 118 was isolated under 1 μM salicylic acid. This mutant featured low background fluorescence, a high signal‑to‑noise ratio, and markedly stronger specific responses compared with other mutants and the MUT1st control group.

    Sequencing revealed that in this favorable mutant, residue 169 of NahR was mutated from glutamic acid to glycine, and residue 248 was mutated from cysteine to threonine. This mutant strain was designated NahR(G169T248) (MUT2nd), exhibiting greatly improved sensing performance.

    Figure 5: Primary screening for high-fluorescence mutants using salicylic acid agar plates

    Figure 6: Secondary screening of high-fluorescence mutants via microplate reader. CK represents the MUT1st control group, DMSO is the control without SA, and 1 μM SA serves as the experimental group. 11, 26, 109, 114, 118, 155, 222, 144 and 252 are the corresponding mutant IDs.

    To accurately evaluate the gain in trace‑detection performance brought about by protein engineering, this study performed fine‑grained characterization of MUT2nd responses to low‑concentration salicylic acid. Using the protein‑mutation‑free MUT1st strain as control, gradient‑response assays were carried out over the ultra‑low salicylic‑acid range of 0–1 μM. The test results showed that MUT1st displayed no significant differences in fluorescence signals within this very low‑concentration window and failed to recognize trace salicylic acid, indicating obvious defects in low‑concentration detection.

    In contrast, MUT2nd exhibited prominent concentration‑dependent response profiles. For treatment groups with 0.01 μM, 0.05 μM and 0.1 μM salicylic acid, bacterial fluorescence signals could not be statistically distinguished from the blank control group, showing weak response intensities. When the salicylic‑acid concentration increased to 0.2 μM, the fluorescence signal of the system rose significantly above the blank background, generating a stably distinguishable specific response. As the substrate concentration kept increasing, fluorescence intensity increased progressively, demonstrating a favorable dose‑effect relationship.

    According to the generally accepted scientific criterion for limit‑of‑detection (LOD) determination (mean background signal plus three times the standard deviation, which covers 99.7 % of random background fluctuations under a normal distribution), combined with gradient-response data, the LOD of strain MUT2nd(G169T248) for salicylic acid was determined to be 0.2 μM. At this concentration, the sensing signal could be effectively discriminated from background noise, enabling reliable trace-level detection of salicylic acid.

    1.4 ARTP‑Mediated Chassis Evolution Realizes Ultimate Upgrade of Sensing Performance

    The MUT2nd strain was subjected to ARTP mutagenesis and screening to obtain the evolved strain MUT3rd. Gradient salicylic‑acid detection results demonstrated that MUT3rd could produce a significant fluorescent signal at an extremely low salicylic‑acid concentration of 0.05 μM. Compared with the original wild‑type sensing system, the limit of detection of strain MUT3rd for salicylic acid was reduced by 2000‑fold, endowing it with ultra‑high trace‑detection capability.

    Module 2: Naphthalene Degradation System

    2.1 Functional Validation of Heterologous Expression for the Aerobic Naphthalene‑Degradation Pathway

    In this study, the recombinant naphthalene‑degradation plasmid pMD18T‑Pnah‑nahABCDEF was successfully constructed, and heterologous expression of the nahABCDEF gene cluster was achieved in Escherichia coli. HPLC results revealed that DH5α strains harboring the degradation plasmid could efficiently metabolize 100 μM naphthalene and produce salicylic acid, whereas no salicylic‑acid product was detected in the blank control group. This confirmed that the degradation pathway can efficiently catalyze the conversion of naphthalene into salicylic acid.

    Figure 9: HPLC detection of the product SA

    2.2 Plasmid Incompatibility Issue and Determination of the Optimal Co‑culture Ratio

    Experimental verification revealed that the high‑sensitivity strain MUT3rd suffered from plasmid incompatibility. It could not stably maintain both the degradation plasmid and the sensing plasmid simultaneously, making the single‑strain detection system unfeasible. Therefore, a coupled co‑culture detection strategy was adopted in this study, and gradient‑ratio assays were performed to screen for the optimal strain combination.

    The OD₆₀₀ of the sensing strain was fixed at approximately 1.0, and the degradation strain was pre‑incubated for 24 h for degradation. Three volumetric ratios of degradation strain to sensing strain (1:1, 1:2 and 2:1) were set up. Fluorescence measurements showed that the system exhibited the strongest fluorescent response and the best detection stability at the strain ratio of 2:1. This ratio was defined as the uniform standard condition for subsequent co‑culture experiments.

    Figure 10: Fluorescence response in co-cultures of sensor and degrading strains with different mixing ratios

    2.3 Screening and Optimization Results of Promoters for the Degradation Pathway

    In this study, the wild‑type Pnah promoter was replaced, and five sets of constitutive promoters with different strengths were screened. Detection results under standardized experimental conditions showed that the degradation strain driven by the P114 promoter possessed the highest naphthalene conversion efficiency, with its relative fluorescence intensity 1.4‑fold higher than that of the wild‑type promoter system. Accordingly, P114 was identified as the optimal degradation‑pathway promoter.

    Figure 11: Relative fluorescence of the biosensor under promoters of varying strengths

    2.4 Screening Results of Degradation Chassis Hosts

    Comparative compatibility experiments among multiple host strains demonstrated that under consistent culture and degradation conditions, the DH5α strain delivered the highest naphthalene conversion efficiency, optimal fluorescence output and superior system stability, making it the preferred host for the naphthalene degradation module.

    Figure 12: Chassis cell screening for the naphthalene degradation pathway

    2.5 Results of Culture‑Medium Condition Optimization

    Multi‑dimensional medium‑screening experiments indicated that exogenous supplementation of Fe²⁺ at a final concentration of 0.5 mg/mL could significantly improve the naphthalene degradation efficiency and fluorescence‑sensing performance of the system, while other tested metal ions, carbon‑source and nitrogen‑source components exerted no obvious optimizing effects.

    Time‑gradient degradation experiments were carried out using the optimized medium system. The results showed that stable and prominent sensing fluorescence signals could be generated after 3 h of degradation with 100 μM naphthalene substrate, which effectively shortened the detection duration of the system.

    2.6 Results of Sensing‑Strain Cell‑Density Optimization

    Gradient experiments of sensing‑strain cell density were performed with the optimal co‑culture ratio and degradation system fixed. The results revealed that obvious sensing performance was achieved when the OD₆₀₀ of the sensing strain reached 0.8. This cell density was therefore set as the standardized detection condition.

    Figure 15: Sensor strain concentration

    By integrating all optimized experimental conditions, a finalized co‑culture detection system was constructed and tested against naphthalene samples with gradient concentrations ranging from 0.01 μM to 500 μM. The results demonstrated that the system achieved a limit of detection as low as 0.1 μM for naphthalene. Featuring a broad detection range, high sensitivity and favorable stability, it can effectively realize accurate detection of trace naphthalene in aqueous environments.
